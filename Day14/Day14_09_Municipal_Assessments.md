# Day 14-09: Municipal Assessments

## Overview

Municipal assessments are charges to homeowners for town-wide improvements like sidewalk replacement or water line upgrades. The key rule is: **if the work is done before closing, the seller is responsible** - even if the assessment comes later.

---

## What Are Municipal Assessments?

### Definition

Charges levied by municipalities for:
- Infrastructure improvements
- Street repairs
- Sidewalk replacement
- Water/sewer line upgrades
- Lead pipe remediation

### How They Work

**Assessment Process:**
1. Town identifies needed improvements
2. Work is planned and budgeted
3. Affected homeowners notified
4. Work completed
5. Costs allocated to properties
6. Payments collected (often over years)

---

## The Responsibility Rule

### The Core Principle

> "If the work is done before closing, even if it's not fully assessed yet, the sellers are responsible."

**Key Determination:**
- When was work completed?
- NOT when was assessment sent
- NOT when payment is due

### Why This Rule Exists

**Benefit Principle:**
- Whoever gets benefit should pay
- Buyer gets new sidewalk/pipes
- If work done after closing = buyer's benefit
- If work done before closing = seller's benefit

---

## Real World Examples

### Example 1: Ridgewood Lead Pipes

> "In Ridgewood here where the lead pipes water lines... they're spreading it out over a certain period of time in the taxes."

**Situation:**
- Town-wide lead pipe replacement
- Costs spread over years
- Some homes done, some pending

**How to Handle:**
- If work NOT yet done: Buyer takes responsibility
- If work IS done: Establish escrow or credit

### Example 2: Sidewalk Replacement

> "I've seen it with sidewalk replacements in towns... the sidewalk work wasn't done yet but it was going to be done after closing. The buyer's getting the full benefit of that, so they have to be responsible for it."

**Key Point:** Work not yet done = buyer's problem.

---

## Handling Unknown Assessments

### When Assessment Amount Unknown

> "If we don't have what the actual assessment is going to be, we develop an escrow at closing or we try our best to find out an estimate."

**Options:**
1. **Escrow at Closing:** Hold funds pending final amount
2. **Credit Estimate:** Agree to approximate credit
3. **Notice Provision:** Buyer accepts responsibility

### When Work Not Started

**For Universal Improvements:**
> "It's going to be your problem. The work's not completed yet, and it's universal - it's going to be every house."

**Approach:**
- Provide buyer with notice received
- Buyer accepts future responsibility
- Document in attorney review

---

## Discovery and Disclosure

### How Assessments Are Found

**Through OPRA:**
- Request municipal records
- Assessment notices on file
- Pending improvements

**Through Tax Records:**
- May show special assessments
- Payment schedules listed

**Through Direct Inquiry:**
- Call municipality
- Ask about pending improvements
- Check for notices sent

### The Multi-Family Warning

> "Notice was sent out on a two-family and instead of sending it... they sent it to the tenant. Luckily the tenant called me right away."

**Risk with Rentals:**
- Town may send notices to occupant
- Owner may not receive
- Still owner's responsibility

---

## Contract Provisions

### Making Sure Boxes Are Checked

> "Just make sure it's checked off. Sometimes these boxes aren't checked off and we don't want to miss them."

**Contract Section:**
- Municipal assessment checkbox
- Seller representation
- Known/unknown assessments

### What Seller Represents

**Standard Language:**
- Seller has no knowledge of assessments
- OR discloses known assessments
- Best of knowledge representation

### Limitation of Representation

> "This is what I know to the best of my belief. I'm not doing research for you. Go do your research."

**Important:** Seller only represents what they know.

---

## Survival of Representations

### General Rule

> "Representations generally do not survive the closing."

**Meaning:**
- After closing, seller's representations end
- Buyer accepts property as-is
- Can't come back for missed items

### The Exception: Fraud

> "The only situations where representation would survive closing even if the contract says otherwise is in the case of fraud or willful concealment."

**Example:**
- Seller received $20,000 assessment notice
- Seller didn't disclose
- Buyer finds out after closing
- Buyer can pursue seller

---

## Putting a Cap on Assessments

### Why Consider a Cap

> "I like to put a cap on because generally if work is not completed before the closing it would be the buyer's responsibility, but I don't want to find out as a buyer when I'm under contract that all of a sudden all the water lines are being replaced and the homeowners are being charged $16,000."

**Scenario Without Cap:**
- Buyer in contract
- Town announces major improvement
- Assessment will be $16,000
- Buyer stuck with full amount

### How Caps Work

**Cap Provision:**
- Seller discloses known assessments
- Unknown assessments up to $X are buyer's risk
- Above $X, buyer can cancel or renegotiate

---

## Due Diligence Steps

### For Buyer's Agents

1. **Request OPRA:** Ask about pending improvements
2. **Review Tax Bill:** Check for special assessments
3. **Call Town:** Any planned improvements?
4. **Check Notices:** Has owner received anything?

### For Listing Agents

1. **Ask Seller:** Any notices received?
2. **Check Mail:** Owner receiving town mail?
3. **Call Building Department:** Planned improvements?
4. **Disclose Known:** Include in listing disclosures

---

## Settlement Options

### Option 1: Escrow

**When to Use:**
- Work is done
- Assessment amount unknown
- Final bill pending

**How It Works:**
- Estimate amount held at closing
- Released when bill arrives
- Balance returned or additional paid

### Option 2: Credit at Closing

**When to Use:**
- Amount is known or estimable
- Work is done
- Parties agree to credit

**How It Works:**
- Seller credits buyer at closing
- Buyer takes responsibility
- Simple and clean

### Option 3: Price Reduction

**When to Use:**
- Large assessment
- Negotiation needed
- Credit has loan implications

**How It Works:**
- Reduce purchase price
- Assessment disclosed
- Buyer accepts responsibility

---

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Known Assessment, Work Done

**Facts:**
- Sidewalks replaced last year
- Assessment: $3,000 over 5 years
- 3 years remaining

**Resolution:**
- Seller responsible
- Credit $1,800 at closing (3 years)
- Or pay off in full

### Scenario 2: Announced, Not Started

**Facts:**
- Town announced water line replacement
- Work starts next year
- Assessment: TBD

**Resolution:**
- Buyer's responsibility
- Disclose notice to buyer
- Buyer accepts risk

### Scenario 3: Unknown Until Inspection

**Facts:**
- CO inspection reveals assessment
- Seller didn't know
- Work done 2 years ago

**Resolution:**
- Seller responsible (work done)
- Research actual amount
- Credit or escrow at closing

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Work Done = Seller Pays:** Regardless of when billed
2. **Work Not Done = Buyer Pays:** They get the benefit
3. **Fraud Exception:** Concealment survives closing
4. **Check the Boxes:** Don't miss contract provisions
5. **Do Research:** OPRA, tax records, phone calls
6. **Multi-Family Risk:** Notices may go to tenants
